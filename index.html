Language models learn from text, demonstrations, and their own attempts at solving problems. These sources usually arrive in separate stages, with different training recipes. Unified training brings them together under one likelihood-based learning rule. The data mixture changes throughout training; the rule stays the same.

Across our experiments, unified training improves reasoning and generalization, preserves more of what the model learned earlier, and produces a stronger starting point for the next task.

01

A common rule for learning from different sources

Pretraining, midtraining, and supervised fine-tuning already share a basic recipe: make the model more likely to produce its training examples. For suitable reinforcement-learning (RL) objectives, the update has the same interpretation, with training targets built from the model’s own responses and their rewards.

For correct/incorrect rewards, Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning (MaxRL) learns from the distribution of correct responses to each prompt. We extend this view to responses from a fixed generator that can differ from the model being trained, and combine the resulting targets with static data in one objective. This supports a shared data format, weighted token loss, and trainer, while generation and optimization run independently.

The central design question becomes: what should the model learn from, in what proportions, and when?

A little more on the theory

With correct/incorrect rewards, the population MaxRL update fits the model’s distribution of successful responses for each prompt. In a finite batch, it averages the correct responses for that prompt; if none is correct, the prompt contributes no update. A different generator gives a different successful-response distribution to fit, generally not the current model’s on-policy MaxRL gradient.

The framework also accommodates REINFORCE and suitable continuous-reward objectives through different targets or weights. It does not claim that every RL objective has this form. Sections 3–4 and Appendix F of the paper give the formulation and implementation.

02

Learn more from the same data

We first train a 124M-parameter model from scratch using general text and a fixed corpus of worked reasoning puzzles. RL asks it to solve new combinations of the demonstrated reasoning operations. We also test larger puzzles absent from both midtraining and RL.

Staged training uses the corpora sequentially before RL. Unified training mixes midtraining data into the final part of pretraining and keeps using it during RL. The best unified configurations achieve higher final accuracy on both reasoning settings, and the advantage extends to unseen larger puzzles.

The strongest unified configurations reuse the midtraining corpus for roughly two or four epochs during pretraining. In contrast, extending staged midtraining beyond its best budget reduces final RL accuracy. More midtraining data during RL can help too, but the preferred amount depends on the task.

Takeaway: timing and composition change how much a model can learn from a fixed corpus. The extra midtraining tokens here come from reuse, not new examples.

03

Count the whole training budget

Mixing more data takes compute. We therefore count pretraining, midtraining, RL generation, and RL updates together, and ask: what is the best performance each approach reaches within a given budget? Both approaches can choose among their tested configurations and stop at an earlier checkpoint.

Staged training is competitive at intermediate budgets. Unified training reaches higher accuracy and coverage at the larger budgets we test. The generalization result is especially clear: within the largest staged budget, coverage on unseen larger puzzles reaches 61.5% with unified training, versus 44.2% with staged training.

Takeaway: the gains are not explained solely by spending more compute. Allowing data sources to overlap can improve how a fixed training budget is used.

04

Preserve earlier learning while developing new skills

What happens to the general capabilities acquired during pretraining? Before RL, unified models remain close to the pretraining-only model on held-out text loss and average general-benchmark performance. Every staged midtraining configuration we tested forgets more on these measures, including the one chosen specifically for its best retention.

We see related benefits with a 0.76B-parameter model and heterogeneous midtraining data containing web text, math, code, and reasoning traces. During mathematical RL, small midtraining mixtures improve held-out corpus loss and accuracy on knowledge benchmarks MMLU and SciQ, while math accuracy stays similar. The tradeoff is lower coding accuracy, even though coding coverage over 32 attempts remains higher than in staged training.

Timing matters here as well. Ending the mixture halfway through RL improves final coding and science-question accuracy relative to constant mixing, but preserves less of the reduction in corpus loss. No single schedule wins on every measure.

Takeaway: a score on the current task is only part of the picture. The mixture also determines which earlier capabilities are retained.

05

Prepare a better starting point for the next task

Does the coding-accuracy cost after mathematical training leave unified models less prepared to learn coding? We continue every model with the same coding RL procedure, without midtraining data.

All four unified models start with lower coding accuracy but finish with higher accuracy than the staged model on both HumanEval+ and MBPP+. The model trained with 5% midtraining data during math performs best on all four final coding metrics: accuracy and coverage on both benchmarks.

The reasoning study shows a similar reversal: the staged model has higher Base accuracy before RL, yet the best unified models finish ahead after RL. Immediate performance does not fully describe a model’s ability to benefit from further training.

Takeaway: evaluate a training decision by what it enables next, as well as by the score it produces now.

06

Study the training process as a whole

These experiments use relatively small models and mostly one RL task at a time. They do not establish a universally best mixture or a scaling law. Some staged and unified comparisons also differ in how generation and training are coordinated; controls using the same trainer help assess the effect of mixing without isolating every implementation choice.

Unified training provides a common formulation and a practical system for studying these choices together. The opportunity is to develop principles for which data to reuse, which responses to generate, and when to change their balance across the lifetime of a model.
